9 Study Techniques Backed by Research

July 14, 2026 · 8 min read

Not all studying is equal. Some habits feel productive but barely move the needle, while a handful of techniques reliably improve how much you learn and remember. The methods below are among the most consistently supported by learning research. None of them require special tools, just a small shift in how you spend your study time.

1. Active recall

Active recall means testing yourself instead of rereading. Close the book and try to retrieve the answer from memory, then check. The effort of pulling information out strengthens the memory far more than passively reviewing it. Turn your notes into questions, use flashcards, or simply cover the page and explain it from memory.

2. Spaced repetition

Reviewing material across spaced-out sessions beats cramming it all at once. Each time you revisit something just as you are about to forget it, the memory grows more durable. Instead of one long session before an exam, spread shorter reviews over days and weeks. Flashcard apps automate this, but a simple schedule works too.

Active recall and spaced repetition are the two heavyweights. If you adopt nothing else from this list, combine these two and your retention will improve dramatically.

3. Interleaving

Rather than drilling one topic until it is perfect and then moving on, mix related topics or problem types within a session. Interleaving feels harder and slower, but it trains your brain to choose the right approach for each problem, which is exactly what exams and real life demand. Study a blend, not a single block.

4. Elaboration

Elaboration means asking how and why, and connecting new ideas to what you already know. Instead of memorizing a fact in isolation, explain the reasoning behind it and link it to examples. The more meaningful connections a piece of information has, the easier it is to retrieve later.

5. The Feynman technique

Explain a concept in plain language, as if teaching a beginner. When you hit a point you cannot explain simply, you have found a gap in your understanding. Go back, fill it, and try again. Teaching forces clarity in a way that highlighting never will.

6. Dual coding

Combine words with visuals. Turning a process into a diagram, a timeline, or a simple sketch gives your brain two ways to store and retrieve the same idea. You do not need to be an artist. Rough diagrams and mind maps are enough to make abstract material more memorable.

7. Practice testing under real conditions

Do past papers or practice questions the way you will actually be tested: timed, without notes, in one sitting. This does two things. It strengthens recall through active retrieval, and it reduces exam anxiety by making the real thing familiar. Reviewing your wrong answers afterward is where much of the learning happens.

8. Distributed practice with focused blocks

Long, unbroken study sessions fade in quality. Focused blocks of 25 to 50 minutes with short breaks keep attention high and fit naturally with spaced repetition. Structure protects the quality of the techniques above; a tired brain does active recall poorly.

9. Sleep on it

Sleep is when the brain consolidates what you studied, moving it into longer-term storage. Pulling an all-nighter trades away the very process that locks learning in. Studying earlier and protecting your sleep will usually beat cramming through the night, both for memory and for how well you perform the next day.

How to combine them

You do not need all nine at once. A strong default is simple: study in focused blocks, use active recall as your main method, space your reviews over time, mix topics, and sleep properly. Layer in elaboration, the Feynman technique, and practice tests as the material gets harder. The goal is not to study more hours, but to make the hours you already spend do far more.
